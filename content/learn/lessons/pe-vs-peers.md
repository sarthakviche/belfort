The **price-to-earnings ratio (P/E)** is the share price divided by the profit per share over the last year. A P/E of 25 means buyers are paying ₹25 for every ₹1 of yearly profit.

**P/E = share price ÷ earnings per share**

A share at ₹600 with earnings of ₹24 per share has a P/E of 25.

## Why a P/E alone says little

Different industries carry very different P/Es. Fast-growing consumer brands often trade at high P/Es; steel makers and banks usually trade lower. A P/E of 30 can be normal in one sector and extreme in another. So the useful question is not "is 30 high?" but "is 30 high **for this kind of business**?"

## How Belfort compares

For companies on its built-in sector list, the research report shows each peer's P/E, return on equity and debt to equity, and the **median** P/E of the group: the middle value, which one odd company cannot drag around.

| Company P/E against the peer median | What the report says |
|---|---|
| More than 1.5 times | "Priced well above peers": a high price leaves less room for disappointment |
| Less than 0.75 times | "Priced below peers": cheap can also mean the market sees a problem |
| In between | Nothing extra |

## Cheap or broken?

A low P/E can mean the market expects profits to fall, has doubts about the accounts, or worries about debt. Check the five numbers before calling a low P/E an opportunity. A high P/E can be justified by fast, steady growth, but it also means a small disappointment can move the price a lot.

## When there is no P/E

If the company made a loss, there are no positive earnings to divide by, and the source returns no P/E. The report says so instead of showing a number.

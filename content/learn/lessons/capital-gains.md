Profit on shares is not one lump. India taxes it differently depending on **how long you held the shares** and **whether trading is your business**. This lesson covers the common case for a swing or positional trader who buys and sells listed shares on the exchange and takes delivery (the shares sit in your demat account overnight or longer).

> This is general education, not tax advice; check with a tax professional. Rates and limits change in Budgets, so confirm the current ones on the Income Tax Department's site before you rely on them.

## The 12-month rule

For shares listed on an exchange, the dividing line is **12 months**:

| You held the shares | The gain is called | Rate (listed shares, STT paid) |
|---|---|---|
| 12 months or less | **Short-term capital gain (STCG)** | 20% |
| More than 12 months | **Long-term capital gain (LTCG)** | 12.5% on the gain above ₹1.25 lakh in the financial year |

These rates and the ₹1.25 lakh limit took effect from 23 July 2024. Before that, the short-term rate was 15%, the long-term rate 10% and the limit ₹1 lakh. Surcharge and cess are added on top, depending on your income. Most swing trades (days to weeks) produce short-term gains. Only a positional trade held past a year becomes long-term.

## STT: the tax you pay on the trade itself

**Securities Transaction Tax (STT)** is charged by the exchange system on each trade, separately from capital gains tax. For delivery trades in shares it is a small percentage of the trade value on both the buy and the sell. The lower 20% and 12.5% rates apply to gains on which STT was paid, which is the normal case on the exchange. STT is **not** deducted when you work out your gain; you simply pay it. The current STT rates are on the exchange and the Income Tax Department sites.

## A worked example (virtual figures)

Asha's year, with made-up numbers:

| Trade | Bought | Sold | Held | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Share A | ₹1,00,000 | ₹1,30,000 | 5 months | STCG ₹30,000 |
| Share B | ₹2,00,000 | ₹3,50,000 | 14 months | LTCG ₹1,50,000 |
| Share C | ₹60,000 | ₹45,000 | 3 months | Short-term loss ₹15,000 |

1. **Short-term loss first.** The ₹15,000 loss on C is set off against the short-term gain on A: ₹30,000 − ₹15,000 = **₹15,000** STCG. Tax at 20% = **₹3,000**.
2. **Long-term gain.** B's gain is ₹1,50,000. The first ₹1,25,000 is exempt, leaving ₹25,000. Tax at 12.5% = **₹3,125**.
3. **Total** before surcharge and cess: **₹6,125**.

Brokerage and similar charges on the sale can be counted against the gain, which lowers it a little; real statements from your broker do this for you.

## When you have a loss

- A **short-term loss** can be set off against **both** short-term and long-term gains in the same year.
- A **long-term loss** can be set off **only against long-term gains**.
- A capital loss cannot be set off against salary or other ordinary income.
- If gains do not use it up, the rest can be **carried forward for 8 years** and set off against later capital gains, **but only if you filed your return for the loss year by its due date**. A late return can put the carry-forward at risk.

So reporting a loss is worth doing: it can cut the tax on a future profit.

## Tax-loss harvesting: handle with care

Some investors sell a losing share to book the loss and offset gains, then buy back. Before copying the idea, remember: every sale and repurchase costs brokerage, STT and other charges; the price can move while you wait; and tax authorities can question transactions that have no purpose except creating a loss. Do not let a tax idea decide what you hold. Ask a professional.

## What this lesson leaves out

Shares bought before 1 February 2018 have special cost rules. Intraday trades and futures and options are taxed as business income, not capital gains (the next lesson mentions this). If you are unsure which applies to you, ask a tax professional.

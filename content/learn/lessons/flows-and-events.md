Some news moves one stock; some moves the whole market at once. A swing trader cannot predict it, but much of it is scheduled, and the dates are public.

## FII and DII flows

**FIIs** (foreign institutional investors, now officially foreign portfolio investors or FPIs) are overseas funds buying Indian shares. **DIIs** (domestic institutional investors) are Indian mutual funds, insurance companies and pension funds. NSE publishes provisional figures for their net buying or selling in the cash market after each session.

| What you may see | What it means |
|---|---|
| FIIs net sellers, DIIs net buyers | Foreign money leaving, domestic money absorbing it |
| Both net buyers | Broad institutional buying |
| Both net sellers | Broad institutional selling |

One day's figure is noise. A run of weeks in the same direction tells you who has been supplying or absorbing shares. It describes the past; it is not a timing signal, and large flows can reverse.

## Scheduled events that move the market

| Event | When | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| RBI monetary policy | The Monetary Policy Committee meets several times a year on dates published in advance | Changes to the repo rate affect banks, lenders, borrowers and valuations broadly |
| Union Budget | Usually 1 February | Tax and spending changes can move whole sectors |
| Results season | The weeks after each quarter ends | Many companies report close together; see the lesson on results dates |
| Major overseas central bank decisions | Published schedules | Can shift foreign flows and the rupee |

The exchanges also publish a corporate events calendar listing board meetings, dividends and other dates for each company.

## Planning around an event

The choices are the same as for results: exit before, hold with a smaller size so a gap stays affordable, or wait until the event has passed before opening new trades. Whatever you choose, decide it before the day, write it in your journal, and remember that a gap can jump past any stop.

## What not to do

Do not trade the event itself by guessing the decision. Even when the decision matches expectations, the market's reaction can go either way. The point of the calendar is to know when risk is higher, not to forecast the outcome.

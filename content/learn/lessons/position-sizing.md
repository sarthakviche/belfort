Most beginners ask "which stock?". The question that decides whether an account survives is "**how much?**".

## The rule

Decide the most you are willing to lose if this one trade fails. Many traders use **1% of capital** or less. Then:

**Shares = money you accept losing ÷ (entry price − stop price)**, rounded down.

## A worked example

- Capital: ₹2,00,000. Risk per trade: 1%, so ₹2,000.
- Entry ₹500, stop ₹480. Loss per share if the stop is hit: ₹20.
- Shares = ₹2,000 ÷ ₹20 = **100 shares**, worth ₹50,000.

Notice that the trade uses a quarter of the capital but risks only 1% of it. The stop, not the rupee amount you put in, sets the risk.

| Stop distance | Shares for ₹2,000 risk | Money invested |
|---|---|---|
| ₹10 (2%) | 200 | ₹1,00,000 |
| ₹20 (4%) | 100 | ₹50,000 |
| ₹40 (8%) | 50 | ₹25,000 |

A wider stop means fewer shares for the same risk.

## Why 1%

With 1% risk, ten losing trades in a row cost about 10% of the account, which is painful but recoverable. With 10% risk, the same streak wipes out most of it. Losing streaks of five to ten happen to every trader, including good ones.

## Two limits the formula does not cover

1. **Gaps**: a gap past the stop makes the real loss bigger than planned. Keep some room for it.
2. **Capital**: if the formula asks for more money than you have, buy what you can afford; your risk is then below the limit.
